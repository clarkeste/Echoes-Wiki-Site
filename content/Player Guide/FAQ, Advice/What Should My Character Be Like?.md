The [[Character Creation Guide|Character Creation Guide]] covers the mechanical *how* of constructing of a [[Character]] in [[Echoes of the Departed]], but the stats of a Character is only a part of them—and perhaps the least unique part. The personality and story of the Character, including their [[Burdens and Backstory|Backstory]], is instead what defines a Character as a unique narrative agent, and is what allows you to leave your own imprint on the story and change [[Hyrule]]—forever.

## Active Characters
It is imperative, that to a certain extent, your Character is *active*. This is in contrast to a Character being passive or reactive. An active character *wants* things, or *believes in things*, and will take steps, large or small, to either get what they want, or to live out what they believe.

By having goals and actually taking steps towards completing those goals, a Character automatically becomes more interesting because they are struggling towards something, pushing against the universe, other Characters, or themselves, to achieve something. This is deeply compelling on a fundamental level, as all stories are built upon conflict—ie, at least two characters who have opposite, mutually-exclusive, or seemingly so, desires and wants. Your Character being active fundamentally means they care about the conflict in the story, and are actively modifying the conflict and progressing it by attempting to resolve it.

Now, while Characters who are active *tend* to be more interesting, this is not always the case. Passive Characters, or more reactive Characters, can absolutely be interesting, or fun to play. Characters also exist on a spectrum. Many Characters are both passive and active. A Character who is on the passive side but who is still fun to play may *receive* orders from someone else, or be forced into *reacting* by someone else, but will still take the initiative on a smaller scale to fulfill their goal. For example, a spy who is simply following orders, but who is active in shadowing targets, hiring informants, or stealing documents.

A passive Character like that may primarily be arms of greater forces like the various factions, but they can still be very interesting to play even if their story is more linear by their own choice. However, a Character who is *so* passive or reactive that they can’t even take steps to fulfill the goals their Character has, is almost always a Character who will not be fun to play or read, and who is poorly-designed.

With a well-designed passive Character, keep in mind you will likely have a different experience than someone with a Character who leans more on the active side. While of course it depends on their exact circumstance, as a Character may be reactive in some contexts and not others, a more reactive or passive Character will usually have more linear stories due to being given objectives or things to do by an outside force, as opposed to being extensions of some sort of intrinsic desire.

Perhaps the most important question to ask with a passive Character is: Ask yourself *why* they are passive; what about the world as it is right now allows them to be passive, allows them to be happy with the status-quo? Are they still working towards something via loyalty to a faction, or are they happy to just sit around in an idyllic village? In which case, that village may become not so idyllic as the story starts and the forces of the world thrust them into conflict.

Regardless, make sure even your passive Characters *want* something; that they have a motivation, which will determine when they should be active, and will guide their actions. Active Characters should have a motivation as well, which may define their actions even more. Perhaps loyalty to a cause, to a hierarchy, to a faction; the desire to find someone, to kill someone. The desire to help wherever possible, or simply the want to make [[Rupees]] and become filthy rich off the backs of the downtrodden and the dispossessed who suffer in the ruin wrought by [[Revolution and Second Civil War|Civil War.]]


## Tension & Conflict
As mentioned in the previous section, Tension, Conflict, or Friction, are essential to any story. Without it, there wouldn’t be one.

Your [[Character]], by virtue of being a main Character and one who will go on [[Adventure|Adventures]] and shape the world to a certain extent, must be in great tension or conflict with someone or something else. Or, they should have values or desires which, even if satiated now, they would be angry if they would be violated in the future, creating ground for story to occur when their happy status-quo is broken.

To a certain extent, this Tension or Conflict is why your Character is in the story at all. Yes, their motivation is too, but if there is nothing blocking them from achieving their motivation, if they could simply wave their hand and achieve world peace or whatever they wanted, then there would be no story. 

Also consider, on a more personal level, what their friction could be with the other [[PC|Player Characters]]. Do they perhaps dislike people they see as arrogant? Do they dislike people of a certain ideological persuasion, or from a certain region? Or maybe they’ve always wanted to be recognized for their talents, and another PC is someone who could do so? Is this desire even conscious? These are the sorts of things which can generate scenes and develop interesting relationships between different PCs, or with [[NPCs|NPCs]]. Just as one person has many different things that drive them, they can have many different motivations at different urgencies at fulfilling different needs, each with different conflicts and tensions. No person is a monolith.

## Backstory
Finally, Backstory. You got a lot of creative freedom to define your [[Burdens and Backstory|Backstory]]—you should feel free to try all sorts of things! As [[The GM|the GM]], I love reading people’s backstories, understanding their [[Character|Characters]], brainstorming concepts with them, or answering questions about the world, setting, or events that would help define their backstory. You should feel free to speak to me about such things if you have any doubts.

But, I understand that often it can feel intimidating, and some pointers or advice can be appreciated. For that reason, I’ve put together some things here that I think elevate a backstory and can make it very fun to play for you, the GM, and the other [[Player|Players]].

#### Determine How It Defined Them
The Backstory of a Character, to a certain extent, defines *why* they are who they are. For example, an arrogant [[Capitalist and Nobleman Class|nobleman]] has likely lived a life of luxury—or perhaps they are compensating for deep insecurity formed during a precarious childhood. If they are a duelist, did they learn the way of the sword through training in peacetime, or during the harsh brutality of war? If it was war, has the bloodshed made them cold and callous, or has it filled them with melancholy and reflection? If it was during peacetime, are they dismissive towards war and its warriors or quietly thankful that they did not become one?

You have an incredibly rich range of options here. It says a lot about your Character about the situations in life they experienced, and in particular *how* they responded to those experiences, and how it shaped them. Character is defined and best shown under pressure and during conflict; what pressured them or caused them issues during life, and how they responded and integrated it into their behavior, helps you understand the Character on a fundamental level and allows you to predict how they will react to things during the game itself.

#### Thread Into the World, if you want!
[[Hyrule]] is a big place, and a wide variety of stories can be told in its cities, farms, and fields. Not everything has to be connected. However, if you do want to connect it to some facet of the story, even in a small way, it can enrich the story by interconnecting various elements and weaving them together into a cohesive whole which can then result in new opportunities for stories and deeper connections.

For instance, connecting a [[Character]] to an existing individual, or an existing event. This doesn’t mean they’re related to that Character familial; it could mean that they want to kill that person, that they had a meaningful interaction with that person, that they had some sort of relationship, etc, etc. 

Think about what gaps you want to fill in [[The Hyrule Historia|the Hyrule Historia]]. Perhaps your Character was a [[Castle Town Parliamentarians|Parliamentarian]], a regular person in [[Castle Town (Historia)|Castle Town]] who was forced to become an outlaw, or to lie low, until the [[Revolution and Second Civil War|Revolution]]. Perhaps your Character loved the monarchy, knew the King or Princess personally, and hate those who removed them from this world. Maybe your Character wants to kill [[Link Steadfast (Historia)|Link Steadfast]], or was screwed over by [[Lakoh (Historia)|Lakoh]], or worked with [[Chancellor Collin Phurn|Chancellor Phurn]].

#### Goals, and Their Place in the World
We talked about this previously in the Active Characters section, but a Character’s motivations are often defined by what happened to them in the past. Think about *why* they want something. Even goals on the smallest level, like gaining the respect of those around you, can often be traced back to something in one’s past; an expectation placed upon them, part of the culture they grew up in, a negative experience, a desire to be in control due to past insecurity, simply copying what you saw others do, etc.

And this applies on a larger scale as well. If your Character is a Revolutionary who truly believes in the cause, ask yourself: Why? What was their place in the world that led them to believe this? Were they personally wronged, or are they perhaps of a more modern, educated class that came to the conclusion in a university that monarchism was wrong? Are they a laborer who disliked growing inequality? Were they a romantic noble who went against the desires of others and the pressures of their society to embrace a fair Revolution of freedom, because they truly believed in it? How did these societal pressures, their place in the world, affect them, shape their personality, and impact their goals? How do they want to change their place in the world, or perceived place in the world, if at all, via their goals?

This is all important because, on an even more fundamental level than before, it threads your Character into the world by making sure they *belong* in the setting.

And, as described previously in the Active Characters section, having goals, motivations, or guiding principles is *essential* in a Character. Even if those goals and principles lend themselves to acting more reactively, such as loyalty to a faction resulting in following orders and going on missions rather than more pro-active behavior on the macro-scale, these goals and principles *define* your Character and must be included, and their causes considered.

#### Daggers, Secrets, and Complications
Perhaps the most fun part of backstories: The unresolved threads which can come back to haunt a [[PC|Player Character]], or the ‘Daggers’ that they can be stabbed with. Essentially, conflict points or conflict-generating facets of your backstory which can be used to cause future tension during the game.

In your Character’s backstory, there should be a variety of things that are unresolved, or which still haunt or pester your Character, or which could re-appear at any time to become dangerous in some way. For instance, perhaps they are being hunted by bounty hunters, or are a wanted criminal, or have a heirloom ring you don’t wish to part with, or are greatly disliked by a certain important person. Even something as small as being discriminated against for being a [[Sheikah]] can count. You can an should have a variety of these, interesting things which can appear during the game and create tension or conflict.

The biggest of these, or the most relevant and most important to your Character, will likely be what their [[Burdens and Backstory|Burden]] consists of. But the most developed Characters often have many ‘Daggers’ in their backstory that they can be stabbed with; not all of high severity, but all relevant or important to them in some way.
