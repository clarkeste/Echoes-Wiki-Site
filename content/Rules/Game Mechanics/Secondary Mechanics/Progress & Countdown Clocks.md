## Progress Clocks & Story Challenges
**Progress Clocks** measure the development of a longer, nail-biting problem. For example, it may measure how close to the top of a mountain the [[Character|Characters]] are, while climbing up its side, or how close a [[PC|PC]] is to picking a lock on an extremely tough vault door. Clocks are essentially Progress Bars—for either you, or your enemy.

When [[The GM|the GM]] creates a Progress Clock, they first decide how many Segments the Clock will have. Actions done by the [[PC|PCs]] to further the goals represented by the Progress Clock will move along its Segments. This can be represented by a successful [[Skill Checks|Skill Check]] ticking it over, or [[Advantage]] being spent on a different task to move the Progress Clock forward towards a goal. 

How progression manifests will depend on the situation. If the [[PC|PCs]] are infiltrating a Castle with the intention of getting to its central Archivery, a Segment of the Progress Clock might be filled each time they get significantly closer to the Archivery, and be filled once they reach it. Progressing would be done by [[Stealth]] Checks, sneaking by guards, getting past locked doors, deceiving sentries—anything that could get them closer to that room.

In a [[Combat]], perhaps the PCs are trying to break down a dam which will flood the area and kill Monsters prone to drowning. In this case, Checks attacking the structure of the dam, analyzing its weak points, would increase the Progress Clock, as the dam gets weaker. Likewise, a Character could spend [[Advantage]] on their [[Attack Check|Attack Checks]] to further the Progress Clock, such as by tossing an enemy or a dropped [[Weapons|weapon]] into the dam, or any other things the [[Player|Players]] could come up with that makes logical sense and of which the GM approves. Of course, if someone picks up several large bombs and throws them at the dam, that may immediately fill all remaining Segments on the Progress Clock.

Not every action taken to progress will necessarily result in a tick of the Progress Clock—however, minor actions will together build up to a tick of the Progress Clock.

When the Clock ticks to its full status, the goal is completed; the dam breaks and washes away the dangerous Monsters, or the archivery is reached, if just barely.

#### Optional Rules
Additional or alternate rules may be added by the GM depending on the nature of the challenge set forth by the Progress Clock.

In some situations, the GM may rule that using the same [[Skills|Skill]], if the situation has not significantly changed, to the same task, may face limitations. For instance, they may say that using [[Athletics]] several times in a row to climb up a mountainside will face penalties or be unavailable, due to the initial [[Skill Checks|Check]] representing their best attempt at moving up the mountain using their Athletics. In these situations, often any Skill can be used so long as a Player can reasonably explain how it is applicable and used.

The most common way this is done is that each use of the same Skill in an Encounter costs 1 additional [[Strain]] each time. For instance, the first [[Skill Checks|Check]] using [[Coordination]] costs 0 Strain. The next, 1. The third, 2. The fourth, 3.

The GM will often make it so that each ‘Tick’ of a Progress Clock results in the situation changing or being twisted in some way, as to keep the challenge dynamic.

If the GM chooses, he may limit who can roll in a row. If there is more than 2 Characters, a single Character cannot roll twice in a row. If there are exactly 2 Characters, a single Character can only roll twice in a row. This is to make sure more people can participate in Ticking the Progress Clock.

## Countdown Clocks
**Countdown Clocks** are the inverse of the Progress Clock. They measure the development of a danger or threat, something which could result in disaster for the PCs. For example, it could represent the Alertness of an Army Outpost while PCs sneak about, the impending arrival of enemy reinforcements, or the quickly diminishing structural integrity of a building which the PCs are fighting inside. As the Countdown Clock Ticks forward, complications build, and get closer to the final consequence of filling out the Countdown Clock.

Like with Progress Clocks, what ticks toward a Countdown Clock depends on the nature of the Clock. For example, if representing how Alert a Base is, failed [[Stealth]] [[Skill Checks|Checks]] or things that alert enemies could Tick it forward. For something like an approaching army, it may tick forward at a steady rate, or from [[Skill Checks]] done by [[NPCs]]. When fighting inside a crumbling building, [[Threat]] or [[Despair]] could knock out supports and cause the building to fall apart quicker. What option used, or what combination, or how much [[Threat]] is required to represent a Tick, is determined by the GM; likewise, progress can be made towards a Tick without necessarily completing it on that action. Consider it as there being additional Clocks that must be filled for one Segment on the main Countdown Clock to be filled, that are unseen to the eyes of the Players.

Countdown Clocks can have any number of Segments, as determined by the GM, largely related to its complexity or related to the amount of steps needed for the danger to become present. In rare cases, the actions of the [[Player|Players]] can lower the Ticks on a Countdown Clock—but be careful: Adversaries or particularly drastic Failures can do this to a Progress Clock, too. In some circumstances, Countdown Clocks can be considered to be Progress Clocks, except for the adversaries of the PCs.

## The Chase and Escape (Competitive Story Challenges)
Whether it be a Ship-of-the-line pursuing a fleeing outlaw, an investigator chasing down a suspected assassin across the rooftops of a bustling city, or a haunted soldier fleeing a Phantom wreathed in eternal night, they all fall under the purview of **The Chase**.

In The Chase, there are two Clocks—a Progress Clock for the PCs, and a Countdown Clock for their pursuers or quarry. When the Countdown Clock reaches completion, the PCs are caught—or perhaps, only one of them—or the pursued adversary manages to escape out of sight.

If the Progress Clock for the PCs is reached, they either manage to escape their pursuers, or catch and tackle the individual they were chasing, depending on what the PCs are attempting. 

In Chases (or Competitive Challenges), it is much more common for PCs to be able to increase the length of a Countdown Clock, or vice versa. It is encouraged that PCs try different things and gambits to try and do this if they so wish, depending on the situation. For example, in a Chase, they may scream at guards up ahead to close a particular gate, forcing the pursued NPC to take a worse route, thereby increasing the Countdown Clock’s duration by 1.

In a chase, the GM may choose to inflict a Strain penalty when a [[Skills|Skill]] is used repeatedly, as per in [[Progress & Countdown Clocks#Optional Rules|Optional Rules]].

Shortcuts can be taken to increase the amount of Segments filled in by a [[Skill Checks|Skill Check]]; but be careful—these options often come with some risk! Feel free to ask your GM about alternative shortcut options, even in Progress Clocks unrelated to Chases.

#### Wagers
In most competitive Progress Clock challenges, and therefore most Chases (unless the GM states otherwise), Characters can make Wagers, forcing them and their opponent to bid. 

A Character must declare a Wager before it is their turn to roll in an [[Encounter]].

On a Wager Check, the [[Difficulty]] of the Check is increased by 2. Then, each Character’s controller decides their bid independently at the same time (usually, the Players states theirs first, if it cannot be done simultaneously for some reason, and then the GM reveals the NPC’s). Their bid can be a number from 0 to 7. Whoever picks the **lowest number** wins the Wager.

The winner of the Wager then makes the Wager Check on their next Turn (the same Turn if the Wager initator won the Wager). The Wager Check is Skill Check the Character was going to make with any relevant Skill, but with 2 additional [[Difficulty Dice]]. They add [[Boost Dice]] **equal to the number they wagered**. This means that if they managed to Wager a higher number, such as 5, they get 5 Boost Dice. If they Wagered a lower number to make sure they’d succeed, such as 1, they would only get a single Boost Die.

If the Check succeeds, they Tick their Progress Clock twice, filling up two Segments. The Encounter then continues as normal, with the opponent making a Check with the usual Difficulty (unless they also choose to begin a Wager). If the Wagered Check fails, the Character who rolled it suffers 2 [[Strain]], and their opponent’s next Check adds 1 automatic [[Success]].

## Stealth and Subterfuge (Story Sequences)
In Stealth situations, Countdown Clocks are commonly used. They represent the Detection of the PCs by the organization or location at large, with the final Tick of the Countdown Clock resulting in them being found and discovered.

Failure on a [[Stealth]] [[Skill Checks|Check]], or a significant Check such as a [[Skulduggery]] Check to pick a door, will usually result in the Detection Clock Ticking by one—but not immediate discovery. Minor actions that don’t require a Check, such as taking something off a table, will add a [[Setback Dice|Setback Die]] to the next Check that could Tick the Detection Clock forward on a failure. This represents, for example, a Guard realizing something is missing and becoming more alert as a result. Additionally, killing someone [[Flanking|from behind]] while hidden for instance, can sometimes only be accomplished if a Tick of the Detection Clock is ‘spent’.

On a Failed Stealth Check, one might assume that the PCs would immediately be spotted. However, just as Progress Clocks can represent tasks that are too big to complete in a single Check, Countdown Clocks (or the Detection Clock in this case) can represent assets or statuses that are too significant to be lost in a single failed Check. This is true for being Detected.

A Failed Stealth Check will usually result in a Guard or Patrol seeing or hearing *something*, which they will come to investigate soon after. It will also result in the Detection Clock being Ticked forward one step. However, it does *not* mean that the Guards suddenly know your location. Only that they grow more alert, and that they may search the source of the sound or glimpse. But, as a skilled spy, you will of course have chances to avoid further Detection—such as by killing or incapacitating the Guards, or escaping out of sight.

Other things, such broken glass on the ground, may cause sound or draw attention when triggered. This will, usually, *not* increase the Detection Clock, but will result in nearby Guards to come near the source of the sound.

#### Determining Detection Clock Size
The amount of Segments on the Detection Clock partially determines how hard it will be to traverse through an area undetected. Various things, such as PC stats, can affect Detection Clock size.

First, the GM, depending on the difficulty of the area and the competence of the defenses, picks either a 4, 6, or 8 Segment Clock. 

Then, increase the Segments by the average [[Stealth]] [[Skills|Skill]] between all [[Character|Characters]] on the PC’s side involved in the operation. This number determines the amount of additional Segments added to the Detection Clock, rounding up. The maximum amount of additional Segments that can be added this way is +4.

#### When to roll Stealth?
Often, [[Stealth]] is not necessary to roll as often as one might presume. If there is no logical way that a Patrol or Guards could see a Character without them being careless, then Stealth is not needed. This is also true if, for example, Characters are moving past Guards who have their backs turned, and are not moving close enough to potentially create audible sound.

Each time this is done, however, and not rolled, one [[Setback Dice|Setback Die]] is added to the next time an allied Character in the [[Encounter]] rolls a Check that, on a Failure, would move the Detection Clock forward.

Many other minor actions that do not require a Check, add this same drawback.

## Travel and Discoveries (Long-term Story Challenges)
The land of Hyrule and the surrounding Kingdoms are large and vast. So are the oceans and the [[Holodrum (Historia)|Empires]] beyond it. Traveling it, depending on the circumstances, can take some time; but may also make sense to do in a more zoomed-out manner, using a Progress Clock.

The Progress Clock when **Traveling** can be increased by Skill Checks done to further it, and represent terrain being traveled. Most usually, these are [[Riding]], [[Survival]], [[Resilience]], [[Vigilance]], or [[Discipline]] Checks. In some cases, Skills such as [[Athletics]], [[Swim]], or [[Operating]] may make sense to be used, as well. Using the same Skills comes with the Strain penalties mentioned earlier; however, breaks or rests at Villages or Taverns can reset the count.

When Traveling, the Players take note of their location, and then set a **Waypoint** that represents their destination. They may then decide which route to take.

#### Costs and Resources
When Traveling, there are few different Resources that should be kept in mind.
- **Supply:** A numerical value which represents water, food, camping equipment, etc. Consume 1 on each Travel Check; some penalties or difficult paths may reduce it by more. 
  
  Heal [[Wounds]] equal to Supply when a Travel sequence ends, representing proper logistics that kept the Characters healthy, and additional medicines or foodstuffs they can consume.
  
  If Supply runs out on a journey, then on every Travel action, Characters suffer 2 [[Wounds]] and 2 [[Strain]] due to hunger, thirst, and exposure.
  
  The maximum amount of Supply that can be carried at any given time is 15.
  
- **Time:** Applicable in situations where time is of the essence. Time starts at a value of 5-20 depending on how much time the PCs have to reach their destination. 
  
  Each Travel Check reduces the Time value by 1; other Travel actions, such as taking a detour to lodge at an Inn, or spending time salvaging Food (to increase Supply), will usually also reduce the Time value by 1. Once depleted, Time cannot be restored.
  
  If Time runs out, the Characters don’t make it in time; whatever would’ve happened at their destination without their presence, happens. In addition to narrative consequences, any time a Character suffers Strain during Travel actions, they suffer 1 additional Strain due to the stress.

- **Rupees:** The same as [[Rupees]] in the rest of the game. Lodgings, purchasing Supply mid-trip, all of these things may cost Rupees.

- **Strain:** Certain actions and Failures on some Checks will result in Characters suffering Strain. Strain is harder to recover when Traveling, and so must be handled with care. If any Character goes over their [[Strain Threshold]], they suffer a [[Critical Injury]], the group loses 2 Time, all Checks made suffer 2 [[Setback Dice]], and the group is forced to Rest at the next opportunity.


#### Actions when Traveling
Just like in regular narrative time, Players are encouraged to come up with things to do, or ideas on how to solve problems, and suggest them to the GM, or otherwise test or ascertain their plausibility. However, these list serves as a general set of examples of things that can be done when Traveling, and costs that may be associated with actions.

- **Travel:** Make a Skill Check to get closer to your destination, and Tick forward the Progress Clock. More detailed rules are in [[Progress & Countdown Clocks#Travel Checks, and Journeying|Travel Checks and Journeying]]. 
  
  Consumes 1 Time and 1 Supply. Sometimes, other paths may be available that fill more of the Progress Clock, take less time, etc, but may be a harder Check, cost Strain or Supply, etc.

- **Foraging/Hunting:** Make a [[Survival]] or [[Culinary]] Check, with [[Difficulty]] determined by the area. 
  
  On a Success, add +1 Supply. Active Character suffers 2 Strain.

- **Cooking:** Make a Culinary Check to [[Potion Creation|cook]] a [[Potion Template - Meal|Meal]] that will lift people’s spirits and be more appealing than eating the rations on their own.
  
  Consumes 1 Supply and 1 Time, but heals 1 Strain on every Character who can eat the Meal. In addition, every Character gains the benefits of the Meal made (Strain or Wound restoration).

- **Rest:** Set up camp or book an Inn and rest for longer than is necessary to sleep, to be ready for the next day.
  
  Consumes 1 Supply and 2 Time. Every Character who rests can roll an [[Difficulty|Easy]] (1p) [[Cool]], Discipline, or Resilience Check to heal Strain equal to Advantages and [[Success|Successes]].
  
  If done in a proper resting area, such as an Inn, Consumes 0 Supply, there is no Difficulty to heal Strain, and in addition to the Check, all Characters resting heal +2 Strain. Inns will have costs, however; commonly 40-100 Rupees for each person.

- **Restock:** At a Village, Town, or City, restock on Supply, most commonly by purchasing more.
  
  Consumes 0 Supply and 0 Time. Add +3 Supply. Costs 300-800 Rupees depending the location, [[Negotiation]] roll, etc.

- **Push Forward:** Travel at an increased pace, pushing the group to their limits to try and get to their destination quicker.
  
  Functions the same as the option named Travel—except that it consumes 2 Supply instead of 1, and all Characters suffer 3 Strain even on a Success, and regardless of any Strain suffered due to a Failure.

#### Determining Clock Size and Supply
The size of the Clock is determined by the distance and conditions in the Traveling. For every 10km between the Starting Location and the Waypoint, the GM adds another Segment to the Clock. For areas with dangerous terrain—such as harsh winterlands, deep deserts, or war-torn battlefields—a Segment is added every 5km.

Supply on all trips is, by default, 5. Two hundred [[Rupees, Currencies|Rupees]] can be spent prior to the Travel at a Village or Town to buy another point of Supply.

#### Travel Checks, and Journeying
Each Skill Check to move forward represents about 10km of travel, or 5km in dangerous terrain, and therefore Ticks the Progress Clock forward.

However, *Failed Checks still move the Progress Clock forward*. Instead, Failed Checks result in every member of the adventuring party to suffer 2 [[Strain]] + the amount of uncanceled [[Failure|Failures]] on the Check. This represents the travel being difficult, costly, or challenges faced along the way.

If there is more than 2 Characters, a single Character cannot roll twice in a row. If there are exactly 2 Characters, a single Character can only roll twice in a row.

Strain is not healed when Traveling, and [[Downtime]] cannot be spent while Traveling. Instead, while Traveling, a Character can heal Strain by taking breaks at Villages, Inns, or pitstops along the way. This will almost always incur some [[Rupees|Rupee]] cost per individual, but will heal at least some Strain. In addition to costing Rupees, this may also cost some time.

#### Discoveries
Discoveries can be made in several ways while Traveling. The first, and most obvious, is that if the PCs’ Path intersects with a notable location or landmark that they would logically see, the GM will describe them coming across it as a result of their Check.

The second is as a result of [[Threat]], [[Despair]], [[Advantage]] or [[Triumph]]. Advantages can result in a structure that is beneficial to the PCs being found, like an abandoned supply cache, or a cave with ores or some other beneficial effect. Triumphs can do the same, to an even larger effect.

Threat and Despair can lead to things that most PCs would consider to be undesirable—such as coming across a Stone Talus, Monster camp, other large enemy, a dark building shrouded in Malice that beckons to them, or a patrol of Follower or Royalist soldiers. Just because this [[Encounter]] occurs due to the presence of Threat or Despair, does not necessarily mean that there are no benefits to the PCs interacting with it. For example, they could steal [[Weapons]] off an encountered Hinox. However, the possible gains should be smaller, and more difficult or costly to recieve.

When a location or thing is discovered, the Travel situation will usually pause temporarily and allow the PCs to have one or more Encounters in regular time to interact with, or explore, their Discovery. Positive Discoveries can often, although not always, result in Strain regeneration.

#### Advantage and Threat
Further Advantage and Threat spending is, of course, allowed, if the PCs choose to spend it on something besides finding a Discovery interesting in the journey. PCs may come up with ideas, or find shortcuts to allow them to fill more Segments in a shorter timeframe, or any number of things. 

Each Threat on a Check can be spent to add 1 Strain to every Character in the travel party. However, the same is not true for Advantages. 1 Advantage can be spent to heal 1 Strain of the Character making the roll, but this can only be done once per Check. 2 Advantage can be used to heal 1 Strain from every Character in the traveling party—again, only once per Check. A Triumph can be spent to heal 5 Strain from the active Character, or 2 from every Character in the traveling party.

#### Better Transportation
The previous rules assume a Character is moving on foot—however, they don’t have to be. Characters and parties moving on horseback, on carriages, or even on ships, move much faster.

When using a better mode of transportation, such as the aforementioned options, halve the amount of Segments on the Progress Clock. Each Check now moves about 20km (10km in difficult terrain), doubling the transportation speed. Additionally, add an automatic [[Advantage]] to all Checks.

When using Trains or similar modes of transportation, there is no need to use these rules—in almost all situations, there will simply be a small time-skip, without the need of Checks, effort, or Strain.
